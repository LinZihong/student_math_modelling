When a longer route buys time:
fixed escape plans for regional mine-flood alarms
Working preprint — October 26, 2026
Abstract
A mine-flood alarm may identify a region without locating the water source or revealing
how long the flood has been spreading. Can a route chosen before the accident still get a
worker out after a delayed warning? We study one fixed route for each starting point and
reported region. The route must reach an exit for every candidate source in that region;
distance is minimized only after this requirement is met. On a tunnel network extracted
from a real mine plan, we evaluate all 1,587 candidate source nodes, all 1,587 starting nodes,
four alarm regions, and three delay targets. For a northwest alarm and a 30-minute delay,
rerouting increases the number of covered outside-region starting points from 720 of 1,243 to
1,200. A 50% detour allowance covers 961, so much of the gain requires substantial extra
walking. Gains occur in all four regions, but differ sharply in size and cost. They also
depend on which water sources and inflow rates the plan is designed to withstand. An exact
finite-scenario search separates cases that rerouting can solve from those requiring better
source information or earlier departure. These are guarantees within an uncalibrated flood
model, not field-validated safety predictions. The study shows how to assess a regional escape
plan by the warning delay it can tolerate and the walking distance that tolerance costs.
1 Introduction
A worker receives an alarm: water has entered the eastern part of the mine. The shortest way
to a shaft runs through that area. Another route is longer, but might remain open for longer.
The worker does not know the exact source of the water and cannot tell how much time was lost
before the alarm arrived. Which route should the emergency plan recommend?
This question differs from finding the fastest route for a known flood. Water travels through
the same tunnels that people use to escape. A route that is short on a dry map may pass through
a tunnel that closes before the worker reaches it. A route calculated for one water source may
fail if the source is somewhere else in the reported region. For a plan to be usable with a regional
alarm, its recommendation must work without that missing information.
We ask how much extra distance is needed to withstand a specified notification delay. For
each starting point and alarm region, we choose a single route in advance. The worker follows
it immediately on receiving the alarm. A 30-minute guarantee means that the route remains
feasible if departure occurs at any time up to 30 minutes after the inrush begins, and that the
worker can then complete the journey. It does not mean that the journey takes at most 30
minutes, or that the worker should wait.
The main finding is that this choice can matter across whole parts of a mine network. In the
CAD-derived network studied here, a northwest alarm leaves 523 of 1,243 outside-region starting
points without a 30-minute guarantee on their dry shortest routes. Changing the route resolves
this for 480 of them. The gain persists at 10- and 20-minute targets. It is not always cheap:
allowing only 20% extra distance recovers 92 of the 480 points, whereas removing the distance
cap recovers all 480. Other regions show smaller gains or require much longer detours.
1

These results answer a planning question, not a hydraulic one. We use a volume-conserving
flood model inherited from a modeling-competition study. The mine geometry comes from a
CAD plan, but the flood parameters have not been calibrated to that mine. The contribution is
to formulate the information available to the worker, compute the shortest route that meets a
regional delay guarantee, and establish where that guarantee is valuable, costly, or impossible in
the resulting scenarios. We do not claim a new general shortest-path algorithm or an operationally
validated evacuation system.
Relation to earlier work.Mine-inrush routing has been studied as an earliest-arrival problem
on a time-varying network [1]. Sensor-placement work asks how soon an inrush can be detected
[2]. Our question connects these two decisions: what should a fixed escape plan prescribe when
detection supplies only a source region and may arrive late? The computational ingredients come
from established time-dependent routing and robust optimization. In particular, first-in–first-out
travel permits arrival-time dominance [3, 4]; selecting among prepared policies after observing
uncertainty is the broader setting ofK-adaptability [5]. Here, the observation is deliberately
limited to the reported region.
The study began as a follow-up to a mine-inrush modeling competition [6]. An initial
competition-network example suggested a benefit from detours. The main evidence in this paper
is the subsequent enumeration of every eligible source and starting node in all four regions of the
CAD network. The earlier example is retained as a secondary comparison in Appendix D, rather
than used to establish how common the benefit is.
2 A mine, a regional alarm, and a fixed route
2.1 The network and the information available
The network has 1,592 junctions and 2,084 undirected tunnels, totaling approximately 248km.
Four shaft-bottom nodes are designated escape endpoints. Reaching the surface after reaching
a shaft is outside the model. The graph was extracted from an anonymized CAD mine plan
by snapping nearby features, splitting intersections, and connecting the shafts. Supplied roof
elevations are used as a proxy for relative floor elevations under a constant-height assumption.
Figure 1 shows the resulting geometry and the four alarm regions.
The medianxandycoordinates divide the network into northwest (NW), southwest (SW),
southeast (SE), and northeast (NE) regions. These are convenient, reproducible partitions, not
verified sensor sectors. Excluding the four endpoints and one auxiliary reference-source node
leaves 1,587 candidate water sources and the same 1,587 worker starting points. Each flood
scenario hasonesource, not all sources active at once. For a regional alarm, its source may be
any eligible node in that region.
The plan specifies a routeP (v,R )for a worker starting at nodev when the reported source
region isR. The route cannot depend on the precise source, which is unreported. It also does
not change with subsequent observations. This is a mathematical model of a region-indexed
route map, rather than a design or human-factors evaluation of physical signs.

2.3 Flood and walking assumptions
The routing calculation needs to know when each tunnel becomes wet and when it becomes
impassable. We generate those times with an event-driven volume-transfer model. Water divides
equally among available level or downhill outlets; if none is available, it enters uphill outlets. A
shallow advancing front covers a tunnel before it transmits water further. Water accumulates in
blocked branches and backs up. Tunnel depth is represented by stored water volume divided by
tunnel width and length. Appendix B gives the rules needed to interpret the schedules.
The main experiments use a constant inflow of0.5 m3/s, a width of 4.99m, a height of 3m,
and a front depth of 0.1m. A tunnel closes permanently when its mean depth reaches 0.3m.
Walking speeds are 1.3m/s when dry, 0.7m/s with the assigned water-flow direction when wet,
and 0.5m/s against it. Speed changes during traversal if the tunnel becomes wet before the
worker leaves. These values specify a test model; they are not measured walking laws or a
validated human-safety threshold.
Water never recedes in this model. Earlier entry into a tunnel cannot cause later arrival at
its far end: this is the first-in–first-out (FIFO) property. Consequently, if a fixed route works
when departure occurs atτ, it also works at every earlier departure. We can therefore test an
entire delay interval by testing its endpoint. The formal argument and the exact travel-time
formula appear in Appendix A.

3.2 Small detours help, but they do not capture the full benefit
For a northwest alarm, a 20% allowance serves 812 origins; a 50% allowance serves 961; doubling
the allowed distance serves 1,162; removing the cap serves 1,200. The median extra distance
among the 480 newly served origins is 49.0%. A study restricted to very small detours would
therefore miss much of the available improvement.
The southwest result has a different cost. Its newly served origins require a median 267.4%
extra distance, meaning a total route about 3.67 times the original length. Increasing the
allowance from 100% to unlimited adds another 175 origins. This is useful as a bound on what
changing routes could achieve, but a poor reason to recommend unlimited walking in practice.
The longest newly feasible outside-region route is about 12.48km. Fatigue, injuries, congestion,
and shaft capacity are not modeled.
The distinction matters for planning. The calculation can show both an economical improve-
ment and a point at which further coverage becomes expensive. It does not prescribe a universal
detour budget. A maximum walking distance, travel-time limit, or other operational constraint
would have to be added before producing a usable plan.
3.3 An example: 254 extra meters change the exit
For a southeast alarm, consider origin P0036 in Figure 3. Its dry shortest route is 6.195km and
ends at P1081. At least one of the 346 candidate source scenarios blocks that route even if the
worker departs at onset. A different fixed route ends at P0986 and is 6.449km long. The extra
254m, or 4.1%, buys a route that meets the 30-minute target forall346 sources.
The improvement is not a tiny numerical change to the same path. About 6.176km of the
original directed route and 6.430km of the replacement are not shared. The two routes have
similar total lengths because different corridors lead to different shafts. This example was selected
after the full experiment to explain the mechanism; the all-origin results, rather than this selected
case, establish its prevalence. Both routes still involve substantial walking.

3.4 Some failures require more than a different fixed route
There are two distinct reasons a common route can fail. First, at least one source scenario may
leave no feasible route at the target delay, even if the worker is told its precise location. Second,
every source scenario may admit an escape route of its own, while nosingleroute works for all
of them. The latter is a limitation of the available information and the fixed-route requirement.
For southwest alarms at 30 minutes, 199 outside-region origins remain uncovered without a
distance cap. Of these, 68 fail even with exact source information in at least one scenario. The
other 131 have a route for every individual source but no common route. Better localization could
potentially help the second group; it cannot by itself resolve the first. We have not optimized a
finer partition or demonstrated how much a real sensor system would recover.
Workers inside the alarm region are also evaluated, rather than discarded. Their results are
less favorable: northwest coverage rises from 14 to 97 of 344 origins; southwest from 31 to 139 of
448; southeast from 25 to 138 of 346; northeast from 28 to 179 of 449. Source and worker may
coincide in this group. Outside-region coverage must not be presented as whole-mine coverage.
Appendix C gives the remaining failure categories.
4 How the routes are computed and checked
The difficulty is to find one path that works in every scenario, not to find a different path for
each source and then compare the answers. We solve this common-path problem exactly for the
supplied finite scenario set, subject to explicit time limits. A timed-out query would be reported
as unresolved, never as infeasible.
6

4.1 Search with several arrival times
For a chosen set of source scenarios, a partial route has one length and one arrival time per
scenario. At a node, we keep these quantities together as a label. Extending the route along a
tunnel updates every arrival time using that scenario’s wetting time, closure time, and walking
speed. An extension is rejected if it is infeasible in any scenario.
If one label has no greater length and no later arrival ineveryscenario than another label
at the same node, the second can be discarded. Any continuation it can use can also be used
by the first, because earlier travel does not hurt. Otherwise, both labels are retained. A dry
shortest-distance lower bound orders the search, so the first complete feasible route is the shortest
common feasible route. The search also discards a label when a reverse, single-scenario calculation
shows that it is already too late to reach any endpoint in that scenario.
There is no approximate cap on the number of labels. The method can take exponential
time in difficult instances; exactness is not a claim of general computational efficiency. Positive
lengths and FIFO travel ensure that an optimal route need not contain cycles.
4.2 Checking all sources without using all of them in every search
A region contains hundreds of source scenarios, but not all need to be active in the search from
the start. We use finite-scenario constraint generation:
1. Begin with the source that is most restrictive for the baseline route, and find a shortest route
feasible for that source.
2. Check the proposed route againsteverysource in the region, using backward propagation of
the latest feasible departure time.
3. If a source violates the target, add it to the search and solve again. If none does, accept the
route. If the search becomes infeasible, report that no common route exists.
This is an exact reduction, not source sampling. At each step, the search solves a relaxation of
the full problem. If its shortest route passes the full-source check, no shorter full-source-feasible
route can exist. If even the relaxation is infeasible, so is the full problem. Each failed check
adds a new source, so the procedure terminates after at most the number of candidate sources,
provided the exact subproblems finish. Appendix A states this argument formally.
4.3 Experimental scope and verification
The primary study evaluates1,587×4×3 = 19 ,044origin–region–target combinations. A
restricted-source comparison repeats those combinations, and a doubled-inflow study evaluates
all origins and regions at 30 minutes, adding 6,348. All 44,436 queries returned a terminal result;
none timed out. Search subproblems had a three-second limit and constraint-generation calls an
overall 30-second limit. The source sets, targets, speeds, and main comparisons were recorded
before the complete CAD outcomes; the higher-inflow redesign is a subsequent sensitivity study.
This is an exploratory computational study, not an independent field confirmation.
We checked the implementation at three levels. On 100 small random graphs with three
scenarios each, exhaustive simple-path enumeration agreed with 400 feasibility and minimum-
length comparisons; a further 100 maximum-delay brackets contained the enumerated optimum.
On CAD subsets, 288 scalar and vector deadline evaluations agreed, and 72 constraint-generation
solutions agreed with the full-scenario solver. Finally, five newly feasible routes per region
were selected by a deterministic ordering and checked by independent scalar forward traversal
against all regional sources, giving 7,935 successful checks. Every accepted nonbaseline route
also underwent full-source backward validation during optimization.
The largest relative volume-accounting residual across the 1,587 flood simulations was below
1.02×10−13. This verifies the accounting, not the realism of the flood propagation. Source

In this particular flood model, multiplying constant inflow by a factor divides all flood event
times by that factor. The doubled-inflow experiment uses this scaling; a direct rerun at source
P0176 reproduced the scaled wetting and closure times exactly in the stored comparison. With
slower wet walking and permanent closure, a route feasible at the larger inflow is also feasible
at smaller positive inflows, for the same source and model. Thus one can design against an
upper inflow bound without requiring the worker to estimate flow during escape. This argument
depends on the model’s time scaling and does not carry over automatically to pumping, receding
water, or different hydraulic dynamics.
5.2 A smaller source set can make most of the benefit disappear
The main source set is deliberately broad: every eligible node in the reported region. As a
contrasting assumption, we retain only nodes within 50m of the supplied CAD water-outline
polygons. Thereare211suchnodesintotal. Theseannotationsarenotverifiedsourceprobabilities
or a geological risk map; the comparison asks how much the result depends on the hazard set.
Now the shortest-route baselines are already high. Northwest gains fall from 480 to 35 origins;
southwest gains from 281 to five; southeast gains disappear. Northeast gains remain, but many
require almost no extra length and amount to choosing a nearly tied alternative. The large
broad-region gains therefore cannot be advertised without explaining the uncertainty they protect
against.
The two source sets are alternative assumptions about the same network, not independent
tests of a universal effect. Narrowing a source set can never make the optimal guarantee worse,
but it can reduce theadvantage over the baseline, because the baseline also becomes safer. This
distinction explains how a better-informed situation can need less route optimization. Whether

6 Limitations and implications
The strongest empirical result is a substantial improvement in coverage across several regions of
one CAD-derived network. It does not establish the same improvement across independent mines.
The four coordinate quadrants are not actual alarm sectors, and enumeration of every source
node does not cover every interior point along every tunnel. Escape endpoints themselves are
excluded as source candidates. Node counts are not survival probabilities, population estimates,
or independent statistical replications.
The main physical limitation is the flood model. It conserves volume but uses equal splitting, a
propagation tree, and whole-tunnel mean depths. It does not resolve momentum, depth variation
along a passage, junction losses, pumping, debris, or water-velocity effects on a person’s stability.
Simultaneous-event conventions can affect a propagation-tree model. Geometry extraction,
estimated elevations, and shaft connectors also need engineering review. The CAD network
supplies a realistic source of geometric complexity; it does not validate the hazard schedules.
The human side is equally limited. Routes terminate at shaft-bottom nodes and ignore
evacuation queues, lift capacity, fatigue, injury, and route-following errors. A 12km mathematical
escape route is not automatically an acceptable emergency instruction. The unconstrained results
identify the available routing margin before such restrictions are imposed. The next operational
step would be to apply defensible walking and access constraints, then measure how much of
that margin remains.
Within those limits, the analysis suggests a useful way to assess a plan. First identify which
shortest routes fail under the intended alarm information. Then determine whether a different
fixed route can meet the delay target, and measure the least extra walking it requires. Finally
separate the failures that need better information from those that need an earlier departure even
with perfect information. This turns a single coverage percentage into a diagnosis of what should
change.
7 Conclusion
In the modeled CAD network, allowing a longer route can turn an unsafe shortest-route assignment
into a fixed plan that tolerates a delayed regional alarm. The gain can cover connected parts
of the network, but its walking cost and sensitivity vary greatly by region. The natural next
questions are therefore specific: how much benefit survives calibrated hydraulic schedules and
walking limits, and how should alarm regions be chosen to reduce the burden of fixed-route
protection? Answering them would move the study from a conditional planning result toward a
defensible emergency plan.

Proposition 2(Finite-source constraint generation).Fix an origin v, target τ, and finite
scenario setSR. Suppose each search returns an exact shortest common feasible route for its
active subsetT⊆SR, or certifies that none exists. If a returned route is feasible for all ofSR, it
attains L∗
R(v,τ). If an active-subset search is infeasible, the full problem is infeasible. Adding a
violated source after every failed full-set check reaches one of these conclusions in finitely many
additions.

Proof. Every full-set-feasible route is feasible forT, so the subset optimum is a lower bound on
the full optimum. If its route passes all sources, it is also an upper bound, and the values agree.
Infeasibility forT excludes feasibility for its superset. A violated source cannot already be inT;
hence each failed check strictly enlargesT, which is contained in the finite setSR.
The statement is conditional on exact subproblem completion. Runtime limits do not provide
an infeasibility certificate. The production records contain no such unresolved queries. Exactness
refers to the combinatorial search; floating-point feasibility checks use tolerances of order10−7
seconds or smaller, not a physical safety margin.
Why a menu indexed only by delay does not extend the guarantee.Suppose the
source set and route constraints stay fixed, and the worker can choose a prepared route using
the exact delay. To cover all delays up toτ, the menu must select atτsome route that works for
every source. That route alone works for every earlier delay by the first proposition. A delay-only
menu therefore cannot make the maximum guarantee larger, although it can spare walking when
an early alarm permits a shorter route. Observations that also narrow the source set, or hazards
that violate monotone feasibility, change this argument.
B Flood schedules and reproducibility
Each tunnel stores volume between zero and its capacityWHℓ, whereW = 4.99m and H = 3m.
A newly reached level or downhill tunnel first receivesWh 0ℓwith h0 = 0.1m. If its far node is
still dry, it becomes a transmitting edge in a propagation tree; if that node is already wet, it
stores further water. An uphill tunnel fills from below before transmitting water to a previously
dry upper node. At a reached junction, incoming flow is divided equally among available level or
downhill outlets (using a 0.005m elevation tolerance); uphill outlets are used when none of those
remains. A saturated dead end causes its feeding edge to stop transmitting and store water.
Source flow trapped behind full tunnels is transferred through them to the nearest node with
storage available; overflow after saturation is included in the volume balance.
Events are ordered deterministically by node and edge identities when simultaneous. Flow
assignment follows the propagation tree, and stored volume never decreases. Mean-depth closure
at 0.3m is recorded separately from the 3m capacity. Walking becomes wet-speed travel when
water first enters the tunnel; the routing model does not resolve a moving front inside it. These
rules explain both conservation and the limitations of interpreting the schedules as hydraulic
forecasts.

D The earlier competition-network evidence
An initial screen used two networks, four regions per network, and eight design plus eight
held-out sources per region. It selected 123 origin–region pairs for closer study. A promising
competition-network case was then expanded to all 113 candidate source nodes in its southeast
quadrant. This was adaptive investigation, not a prespecified external confirmation.
With the same slower walking speeds used in this paper, all 548 outside-region origins were
evaluated at three delay targets. At 30 minutes, dry shortest routes covered 268 origins, a 20%
allowance covered 501, and unlimited distance covered 545. This supports the possibility of a
substantial routing benefit in a different graph, but it covers only one region and cannot replace
the four-region CAD analysis.
The earlier study also exposed the danger of a small scenario set. A route designed for
eight sources and successful on eight held-out sources supported only about 90 seconds when a
previously omitted node source was included. Redesigning against the full set repaired that case.
Twenty-four additional tunnel-midpoint sources were checked for one selected competition route,
but this limited check does not certify continuous source coverage for the CAD study. These
results motivate exhaustivenodeenumeration and an explicit statement of what remains outside
it.
