Delayed departure can reverse the exposure benefit
of a mine escape connection
Working preprint — October 25, 2026
Abstract
An additional tunnel gives evacuees more route choices, but also gives floodwater another
route. Can the same connection reduce flood exposure for an early evacuee and increase it for a
late evacuee, even when each chooses the best available route? We construct a low-lying tunnel-
network motif in which this reversal occurs under two different hydraulic models. An added
connection initially diverts water away from an existing escape route, then feeds accumulation
at the worker’s origin. A simple origin-depth lower bound certifies late harm independently of
route optimization: the worker already encounters more water at departure than an explicit
escape trajectory encounters anywhere in the original layout. In a locally frozen 16-condition
factorial, 12 conditions meet a prespecified 2cm early-benefit/late-harm criterion in both models.
All 16 admit the late-harm certificate in both models at three walking speeds. Refinement,
junction-storage changes, and depth-responsive walking preserve the sign in the tested cases,
although not every sensitivity retains a 2cm margin. The result is a mechanistic modeling
counterexample, not a calibrated mine safety prediction. It shows why evaluating an escape
connection requires recomputing the hazard field and specifying the departure-delay regime,
rather than comparing route lengths alone.
1 Introduction
A mine escape passage can carry both people and the water from which they are escaping. This
creates a difficulty that a shortest-path map cannot resolve: changing the walking network changes
the hazard itself. A connection that appears beneficial on a dry map may redirect water into a
previously sheltered part of the mine.
Mine water inrush is the sudden entry of water into underground workings, for example when
excavation intersects a water-bearing formation or a flooded void. Water spreads through connected
roadways, follows elevation gradients, and can accumulate behind restrictions or in depressions.
An evacuation instruction reaches workers only after detection, interpretation, communication,
and response. The relevant comparison is therefore not just between routes, but betweenlayout–
departure-time pairs. Published mine-evacuation research already couples evolving water conditions
to route planning [1]; three-dimensional tunnel studies also identify cross passages as important
channels for both water and escape [2].
This study asks a narrower question:
Can opening a connection improve early escape exposure but worsen late escape exposure,
even after allowing the evacuee to change routes?
We answer yes within a specified family of hydraulic models and tunnel geometries. The mechanism
is a competition between diversion and accumulation. Initially, the connection diverts part of an
1
inrush away from an existing downstream escape route. A worker who leaves promptly can benefit
without using the new connection. Later, the same connection supplies a low-lying origin. Once
that origin is deep enough, no route choice can undo the exposure already present at departure.
Our principal contribution is the combination of a reproducible counterexample family and an
elementary but useful certificate of this mechanism. We compare two hydraulic treatments, retain
their disagreements, and distinguish numerical verification from physical calibration. The certificate
is stronger than a comparison between two routing heuristics: its lower bound applies to every
new-layout trajectory starting at the prescribed origin and time, including trajectories with waiting
or repeated vertices. Its upper bound needs only one explicit old-layout escape trajectory.
The investigation began as a follow-up to the 2025 CUMCM mine-inrush routing problem [3].
That problem motivates network representations and a baseline inflow of0.5 m3 s−1. Here we change
the research question from routing within a fixed network to evaluating a network alteration. The
study below is self-contained; it does not require the competition solution or its data. We use
an idealized motif to isolate the mechanism. Supplied competition and CAD-derived networks
motivated the setting, but neither is presented as a calibrated hydraulic case study.

The worker’s origin is lower than bothA and C. In the original layout, water can reach that
depression only through the longer route viaC. AddingAB creates a more direct downhill supply
from the inrush. It also diverts flow away from other branches. These two hydraulic effects act on
different timescales. A prompt evacuee can leave the depression before substantial accumulation; a
late evacuee starts in the accumulated water.

mines.

4.2 SWMM comparison
The second calculation uses the United States Environmental Protection Agency’s Storm Water
Management Model (SWMM), accessed throughswmm-toolkit 0.17.0, with dynamic-wave routing
[6]. The same links, elevations, cross sections, roughness, and source are supplied, but SWMM uses
its own junction and outlet treatment. The source enters a junction andO is a free outfall. The
main factorial uses 6.25-meter maximum segment length, a fixed 0.125-second routing step, and
two-second output intervals.
The two calculations are not independent samples from a distribution of true models. They
share depth-averaged physics and idealized geometry. Agreement is evidence of robustness to these
two implementations and their junction treatments. Disagreement is also evidence and is reported
below.

5.2 Route envelopes and continuous witness peaks
The path comparison interpolates the saved depth field in space and time, permits depth to recede,
and does not permanently close a tunnel at its first threshold crossing. The main factorial samples
trajectories at intervals of at most one second. It includes the origin, all segment endpoints, and
arrival. Departure-grid results elsewhere use two-second increments and explicitly censor arrivals
beyond the one-hour horizon.
For the certificate, we removed trajectory sampling error relative to the saved reconstruction.
In one space–time cell, bilinear depth interpolation composed with constant-speed motion is a
quadratic function of time. Split the trajectory whenever it crosses a spatial knot or an output
time. On each resulting interval, evaluate both endpoints and any interior concave quadratic vertex.
Taking their maximum gives the exact peak for that reconstruction, up to floating-point arithmetic.
This calculation is exact for interpolated output, not for the unknown continuum hydraulic
solution. The distinction matters: spatial refinement and hydraulic time-step checks remain necessary.
We verified the evaluator on a fieldh(x,t ) = x(1−t), whose trajectoryx = t has the interior
maximum1/4, and on its reverse traversal.

confirmation result is local to its stated parameter family.
7 Interpretation and limitations
The result is a failure of fixed-hazard network intuition. On a graph with unchanged edge conditions,
an extra option cannot worsen an optimal route. Here the added connection changes the conditions
on existing routes and at the origin. Early diversion and later accumulation can have opposite
effects even when the worker uses the same escape path.
This does not imply that removing mine connections is a safety strategy. Cross passages can be
indispensable for other inrush locations, fires, ventilation, rescue access, and congestion. Nor does
the experiment optimize a door-closing schedule or establish that a water barrier can be operated
safely. It identifies a quantity that a layout assessment should examine: whether a connection’s
hydraulic effect raises origin exposure during a plausible response delay.
Several boundaries are substantial.
1. No field calibration.The geometry is idealized, the inflow is prescribed, and the junction
treatments are approximations. Neither model is validated against measurements of the
proposed motif or an operating mine. The credible component is the stated conservation-law
model and its numerical checks; the study does not establish predictive accuracy at a site.
2. Depth is not complete hazard.Velocity and human stability are omitted from the objective.
A lower peak depth need not mean a safer journey if the current is faster. The conclusion is
specifically about depth exposure.
3. A local network family.All confirmation cases share a low-lying origin and one topology.
They establish persistence under several parameter changes, not prevalence across mine layouts.
Large junction storage and alternative drainage may mitigate or remove the effect.
9
4. Numerical uncertainty remains.The certificate is exact only for reconstructed outputs.
Refinement preserves its sign in selected cases but does not supply a rigorous PDE error
bound. SWMM’s dry-front discrepancy and the models’ threshold disagreements are material
qualifications.
5. The early comparison has a restricted policy class.It enumerates simple paths at a
fixed speed. The late certificate has broader scope, but it still prescribes the worker’s location
and departure time. Advance relocation and a different warning policy change the problem.
The practical lesson is therefore methodological: evaluate the hydraulic and evacuation effects
of an added connection together, and report the departure regime. A shorter dry route and an early
favorable simulation do not settle the late-evacuation comparison.
8 Conclusion
An added tunnel can initially shield an escape route by diverting water, then increase unavoidable
exposure by supplying the worker’s low-lying origin. The tested reversal survives two hydraulic
treatments and a frozen parameter study, while its magnitude and some threshold classifications
remain model dependent.
The next question is whether the origin-depth certificate can guide selective, field-informed
analysis of larger networks. Candidate connections could first be screened for short hydraulic access
to occupied depressions, then checked with measured junction geometry and transient observations.
A second question is whether detection, relocation, or controlled hydraulic isolation can preserve
early benefits without creating late accumulation. Answering either requires information beyond a
dry connectivity map.
