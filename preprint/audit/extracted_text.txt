When Does a Slower Escape Route Buy Useful Time?
Departure-Delay Guarantees and Small Route Menus
in Flooding Networks
Working preprint — October 25, 2026
Abstract
An escape route optimized for immediate departure need not remain usable after a
delayed notification. We study the cost of choosing a fixed route that remains feasible over a
specified departure-time interval, and the related problem of preparing a small menu of routes
for different flood locations. For prescribed FIFO tunnel-arrival functions with permanent
closures, intervalfeasibilityreducestooneendpointcondition. Atwo-departurelabelalgorithm
minimizes nominal arrival time subject to that condition; a backward calculation supplies
infeasibility certificates and bounds for a travel-budget search. Small route menus are selected
by exact maximum coverage within a training-derived candidate set and evaluated at withheld
flood locations. Experiments use a competition network and a CAD-derived network, with a
volume-accounting flood surrogate. Allowing 5% extra nominal travel improves unrestricted
departure tolerance at 63 of 80 sampled origins, but improves tolerance within a 60-minute
horizon at none of them. Three-route menus increase held-out timely coverage from 94.77%
to 96.56% on the competition network and from 97.26% to 97.89% on the CAD network,
relative to a single nominal route. These modest gains, together with bottleneck certificates
and sensitivity tests, show why a large mathematical delay margin need not be operationally
useful. All numerical findings are conditional on the surrogate hazard schedules; they are not
calibrated mine-safety predictions.
1 Introduction
Suppose an evacuation plan tells a miner to take the currently fastest route. The notification may
arrive late, preparation may take longer than expected, or the assumed location of a water inrush
may be wrong. Recomputing a shortest path after every change is one response. A different
question arises when routes must be communicated or prepared in advance: how much immediate
travel efficiency should be sacrificed to keep a fixed plan usable?
This study starts from the mine-inrush problem in the 2025 Contemporary Undergraduate
Mathematical Contest in Modeling (CUMCM), Problem D [1]. The competition specifies tunnel
networks, water-inrush scenarios, and notification times, and asks for flood propagation and
escape plans. Its fastest-route formulation motivates the follow-up question studied here:
Given a bound on extra nominal travel time, how much additional departure delay can
a fixed route tolerate, and how much of that tolerance falls within a relevant planning
horizon?
We also ask whether two or three precomputed routes can cover several flood locations. These
questions concern fixed routes and small route sets, rather than a new shortest-path calculation
for each realized situation.
Three distinctions are essential. First, the latest feasible departure along a route is a model-
relativeavailability deadline, not a recommendation to wait underground. Second, a route that is
1
fastest at a delayed departure need not minimize travel at the nominal departure while remaining
feasible later. Third, the existence of a usable route in a menu does not imply that a miner can
identify it from locally available information.
Our contribution is a precise formulation and reproducible investigation of these distinctions.
We give an exact constrained-routing method, a certificate for when rerouting cannot increase
delaytolerance, andafinite-scenarioroute-menuconstruction. Theexperimentsrevealalimitation
of the initial motivation: unrestricted delay gains can be dominated by very late modelled closures.
We therefore report both unrestricted and horizon-capped improvements, including null results.
Relation to existing work.FIFO time-dependent shortest paths and the role of waiting have
a substantial literature [2, 3]. Integrating time-dependent speeds, rather than freezing a travel
time at entry, is an established way to preserve FIFO behavior [4]. Our label dominance argument
specializes the multi-label principle used in multicriteria shortest paths [5]; it is not a claim to
have invented a new general label-setting paradigm. Bounding deterioration of nominal quality
while improving robustness is closely related to light robustness [6]. Recoverable robust routing
instead permits a route to be updated after disruption [7]; the main optimization here keeps
the route fixed. Route-menu selection is finite maximum coverage, whose submodular structure
is classical [8]. The contribution lies in the problem specification, the exact two-departure
implementation, and the empirical separation of mathematical margin, relevant margin, and
menu availability.

Theorem 1(Correctness).The two-departure algorithm returnsC(d)and a route attaining it,
or certifies infeasibility. No fixed cap on the number of labels is required for correctness.
Proof. Every label represents one common path prefix, so an exit label is feasible at both
departures. By FIFO, componentwise dominance is preserved under every common suffix.
Removing a dominated label cannot remove a strictly better feasible complete route. The
backward bound discards only prefixes from which no suffix can finish.
A prefix containing a cycle is dominated by its prefix before that cycle, or by a retained
label dominating that earlier prefix. Thus cyclic extensions cannot be necessary, and the finite
set of simple paths bounds the possible nondominated labels. The search terminates. Finally,
positive travel times make the nominal coordinate increase along every extension. If a better
exit route existed when the first exit label was extracted, a retained prefix of that route would
have a smaller nominal key and would have been processed first. This contradicts the extraction
order.
The number of nondominated labels can be exponential; we make no polynomial worst-case
claim. The experiments use neither a label cap nor approximate dominance. In 260 extended-
range queries, the maximum number of simultaneously retained labels at a node was 17.

5 Data, hazard schedules, and experimental design
5.1 Two networks
The competition case uses Attachment 2 of CUMCM 2025 Problem D: 663 vertices, 977 tunnels,
and approximately 125.62 km of tunnel centerline. Its supplied inrush, exits, and three miner
locations define the base case. We insert the inrush in its tunnel before simulating water. The
miners are inserted into the routing graph only afterward; this prevents a change in observation
locations from changing the flood simulation.
The second case uses a previously extracted, anonymized CAD network with 1,591 vertices,
2,083 tunnels, and approximately 247.98 km of centerline. The extraction uses 2,347 roof-elevation
annotations, inverse-distance interpolation within 300 m, and a median wall-based width estimate
of 4.99 m. Four shaft bottoms are designated as exits; travel ends there and excludes ascent
to the surface. The base inrush is placed at a tunnel touching a mapped water-accumulation
outline, selected by proximity to the shafts along the network. Four illustrative miner positions
are inherited from the prepared case.
The CAD extraction joins endpoint gaps below 8 m, splits planar intersections, keeps the
largest component, and adds 10–66 m shaft connectors. These rules may create or omit important
connections. Roof elevations are used as a proxy for bed elevations; under the assumed constant
3 m tunnel height a uniform offset would preserve relative elevations, but actual heights are not
established. The geometry has not received an independent engineering audit.
6
5.2 A volume-accounting surrogate, not a hydraulic validation
The routing experiments use a supplied event-driven storage-and-transfer model, described fully
in Appendix A. It assigns volume to tunnels, initially advances a shallow front, divides flow
among eligible outlets, and backs water up when downstream capacity is exhausted. We use
Q = 30m 3/min, height 3 m, advancing depth 0.1 m, and a mean-depth closure threshold of 0.3
m. The competition width is 4 m; the CAD width is 4.99 m. These scenario parameters do not
represent measurements of an actual accident.
Unlike the original competition implementation, which simultaneously stored and retransmitted
the same inflow, the surrogate accounts for stored volume and overflow. The maximum relative
volume residual in the two base runs was below3.2×10−15. This verifies bookkeeping, not water
pressure, hydraulic gradients, or local depth. In particular, equal splitting, a parent-tree transfer
convention, and mean-depth closure can produce long or inaccurate hazard times.
Simultaneous events are processed in a stated lexicographic edge order. We canonicalized
that order to remove dependence on workbook insertion order. Reversing the convention is a
separate model sensitivity, not a different numerical tolerance. It changes some CAD closure
times substantially; Section 6.3 reports the effect.
5.3 Origins, budgets, and withheld source locations
In addition to the seven illustrative origins, we sample 40 non-exit vertices per network without
replacement, with seed 20261025. Sampling is uniform over vertices, not tunnel length, occupancy,
or worker exposure. These 80 origins are the primary denominator for aggregate single-source
comparisons. All are feasible att0 = 1min in the base schedules.
We evaluate nominal travel budgetsϵ= 0, 0.01, 0.05, 0.10and horizons H = 60, 180min, as
well as the unrestricted deadline. Improvements below one second are treated as numerical/non-
substantive for counts; optimization itself is not rounded to seconds. The horizons are diagnostic
design choices introduced to interpret the exploratory results, not operational standards or
preregistered hypotheses.
For the menu experiment, each network has 16 source nodes: the base source plus 15 vertices
sampled independently of outcomes using seed 20261026, excluding the base source and exits.
The first eight sources are used for training and the other eight for testing. Each is paired with
departure times 1, 15, 60, and 180 min. Thus every origin has 32 training and 32 test cells.
Source locations are disjoint across the split. Scenario-optimal routes from test sources never
enter candidate generation or menu selection.
The reported menu proportions pool test cells over the 40 sampled origins per network,
conditional on an unrestricted scenario-optimal route existing. This gives 1,280 eligible cells on
the competition network and 1,279 on the CAD network. Excluded infeasible cells are disclosed
in the result files. Cells sharing a source are dependent, and the selected graphs are not a
population sample; no binomial confidence intervals or population-level reliability claims are
made.

6 Results
6.1 Large unrestricted gains need not buy useful time
Table 1 shows why a single headline gain is inadequate. In the competition case, the first two
illustrative origins reach their maximum modelled departure deadline with at most 5% extra
nominal travel. However, the deadlines occur many days after the inrush. In the CAD case,
M1 has a much shorter deadline of 4.663 min; it is already attained by the nominal route, so
Corollary 1 proves that rerouting cannot extend it in this schedule.
For CAD M2 and M3, the maximum feasible departure is later than the deadline of the
nominal route, but a 5% travel budget does not unlock it. The extended cost curves in Figure 2
expose the distinction between an available alternative and an affordable alternative. At the
extreme, the sampled curves require about 270% extra nominal travel for M2 and 106% for M3
near their maximum deadlines.
Among the 80 sampled origins, 31/40 competition origins and 32/40 CAD origins gain more
than one second in unrestricted deadline under the 5% budget. AtH = 180min only one sampled
origin benefits; atH = 60min none does (Figure 3). This is not evidence that delay tolerance is
generally useless. It shows that the same output can look impressive or irrelevant depending on
whether its gain occurs within the decision horizon.
The informative positive case is competition origin S31, node P0429. Its nominal travel is
26.476 min and its original route’s deadline is 72.653 min. A route adding approximately 4.23 s
of nominal travel extends the modelled deadline to 886.555 min. The unrestricted gain exceeds
13 hours, but the gain relevant to a 180-minute horizon is 107.347 min, and that relevant to a
60-minute horizon is zero. The large ratio should not obscure these three different quantities.
The algorithmic distinction also matters: over 260 extended-range queries, the route optimized
at the delayed departure can cost up to 2.247 additional nominal minutes compared with the
two-departure optimum. This is a comparison of objectives, not a claim of improved flood
prediction. The largest observed number of labels retained at a node is 17; these small instances
do not remove the exponential worst-case possibility.

The menu result supports a limited interpretation: a few precomputed alternatives can improve
the availability of a reasonably short route across the tested source locations. It does not establish
that three routes are sufficient for all locations, nor that their correct selection is possible without
information about the event. Because selection is scenario-aware in the evaluation, coverage is
an upper bound on what an unspecified local decision rule would achieve.
6.3 Sensitivity and model dependence
We vary inflow to 20, 45, and 60 m3/min using the verified single-source scaling, hold all wet
walking speeds at 1 m/s as a direction-insensitive variant, and reverse the simultaneous-event tie
convention. For the 40 sampled competition origins, the unrestricted 5%-budget improvement
count remains 31; for the CAD origins it remains 32. In the competition case, increasing inflow
to 45 or 60 m3/min brings one improvement into the 60-minute horizon. All tested CAD variants
still yield zero sampled improvements within either 60 or 180 minutes.
These aggregate counts are more stable than individual hazard times. Reversing the event
convention changes some CAD closure times by as much as 38,825 s (about 10.8 h), despite
volume accounting and unchanged short-horizon improvement counts. Canonical ordering makes

a run reproducible; it does not resolve the physical ambiguity of simultaneous merging fronts.
A calibrated hydraulic model could change the conclusions. The menu experiment uses the
canonical base convention and has not been repeated over all these hydraulic variants.
7 Discussion and limitations
The initial question suggests a simple recommendation: accept a slightly longer route in exchange
for more time to act. The results require a more selective answer. Extra nominal travel buys
useful tolerance only when three conditions coincide: the baseline deadline lies within the
relevant horizon, another route actually extends that deadline, and that route is affordable under
the nominal travel budget. Failure of any condition makes a robustness improvement either
impossible, irrelevant to the chosen horizon, or too expensive.
This interpretation also clarifies the role of precomputed routes. A menu can be useful even
when a single universal route is not. But its value depends on reusable routes across scenarios and
on an information mechanism for selecting among them. The experiment measures the first issue
and deliberately leaves the second open. A natural next step is to define observable categories of
inrush location and choose one route robust to each category, then evaluate a complete lookup
or switching policy on held-out events.
Several limitations prevent safety claims. The flood surrogate is not a pressure-driven hydraulic
solver; local sloping-tunnel depths, drainage, flow reversal, seals, doors, and flooding at shaft
facilities are not validated. A tunnel is treated as uniformly wet once water enters it, and its
closure uses average stored depth. Direction-specific wet speed is frozen to a reference direction
even when later inflows merge. Canonical event priority is an explicit modelling convention.
The CAD topology and interpolated roof elevations may be wrong at precisely the connections
controlling a detour.
The experiments use one base inrush per network for delay optimization and only eight
held-out source nodes per network for menu evaluation. Uniform vertex sampling overweights
densely segmented parts of a drawing. The high nominal menu baseline indicates that many
designed scenarios are not strongly discriminating. Other source distributions, heavier inflows,
or different delay ranges could produce different gains. Scenario counts are not independent
accident observations. The chosen 60- and 180-minute horizons illustrate interpretation, not
emergency-response standards.
Finally, even the exact routing results are conditional guarantees: they concern following one
route under one prescribed family of arc functions. They omit the safety of remaining at the
start before departure. An availability deadline must never be interpreted as authorization to
delay evacuation.
8 Conclusion
We formulate escape planning as a choice of how much nominal travel to exchange for a guaranteed
departure interval. FIFO reduces the interval to its endpoint, but optimizing nominal arrival
under that constraint generally requires two clocks. Backward deadlines provide an impossibility
certificate; a constrained label search and bisection quantify the trade-off. Finite route menus
extend the question to multiple flood locations without inventing accident probabilities.
The experiments show why the interpretation of robustness matters as much as its optimization.
Unrestricted gains are common in these surrogate schedules, yet gains within a one-hour departure
horizon are absent in the base samples. Small menus improve held-out timely coverage modestly,
while a training-optimal single route can overfit. These findings support selective precomputation,
explicit horizons, and honest information assumptions rather than a universal prescription to

This appendix specifies the simulator sufficiently to distinguish its bookkeeping from hydraulic
assumptions. Each undirected tunnel has volumeVe, current storage rate, a reference inflow
endpoint, and a mode: dry, covering, through-flow, filling, uphill ponding, full, or full conduit.
1. A wet node divides its incoming flow equally among nonfull outlets, excluding the tunnel
currently feeding it. Level/downhill outlets are used first; uphill outlets are eligible only
when there are no eligible level/downhill outlets. Elevation differences within 0.005 m
count as level.
2. A dry level/downhill outlet stores flow until its volume reachesWLh 0. If it is fed from one
endpoint and its far node is still dry, it becomes a through-flow link, wets the far node, and
records a parent relation. Otherwise it continues to store water. An uphill outlet ponds
until full before wetting its upper endpoint.
3. A through-flow link transfers its assigned flow without increasing its own volume. If its
downstream node has no outlet, that link starts storing water instead. This separates
storage from retransmission and removes the original double counting.
4. When a tunnel is full it no longer accepts storage. A source trapped behind full links is
reassigned through full links to the nearest node with available storage or a backing-up
parent; if none is reachable, further input is recorded as overflow. This is an algorithmic
redistribution rule, not a hydraulic-head calculation.
5. Between events, storage rates are constant. The next event is the first arrival at a
volume target: shallow coverageWLh 0, mean-depth closureWL (0.3), or capacityWLH.
Simultaneous events use the canonical edge ordering. Rates are recomputed after state
changes.

Closure is recorded when the mean-depth threshold is first reached. Routing begins wet-speed
travel at the first entry of water, not at complete shallow coverage. This intentionally conservative
spatial simplification is still not a substitute for local water depths.
For a single constant source, all rates are proportional toQ, while event targets are fixed
volumes. With a fixed tie convention, multiplyingQ byλ>0preserves the event sequence and
divides all event times byλ: induct on successive target-volume events. This scale property
justifies the inflow perturbations used here. It does not extend unchanged to multiple sources
with unscaled activation delays or time-dependent drainage.
Flooding is run on the graph split only at the inrush. Origins inserted afterward inherit
the original directed arc schedule on each subarc. This preserves exact traversal through those
observation splits and avoids changing storage dynamics merely by adding a miner’s location.
It does not establish invariance of the flood surrogate to arbitrary remeshing of the underlying
CAD network.

B Verification, numerical conventions, and reproducibility
Theaccompanyingdirectorycontains code/, data/, results/, andfigures/. Thetwo-departure
method is inrobust.py; run.py produces budget results;diagnostics.py checks scaling and
model variants; menus.py generates the source-split menu experiment. The manuscript tables
and figures are generated from those outputs, rather than transcribed from a prior draft.
Five new tests include exhaustive comparison of 1,200 constrained queries on 200 random
directed graphs, comparison of reverse and budget deadlines with all simple paths, and exact
small-menu examples. Ten inherited tests cover single tunnels, series, forks, loops, elevation
rules, multiple sources, volume accounting, FIFO, inverse arrival maps, and shortest paths versus
enumeration. This is algorithm verification, not validation against observed flooding.
Closure comparisons use a10−9s numerical allowance inherited from the routing model;
reverse-bound pruning uses10−8s. Label dominance uses direct componentwise comparisons
without heuristic tolerances. Budget bisection uses a 0.01 s final bracket and a10−8s objective
allowance. Gains counted in tables exceed one second, far above these allowances. Raw paths
and lower/upper deadline brackets are retained.
The additional 40 origins in each network are stored explicitly, as are all menu source nodes
and train/test assignments. Input and source-code SHA-256 hashes identify the computation.
Portable JSON routing schedules permit routing results to be inspected independently of the
flood implementation. Pickle caches are local acceleration files and are not required for a fresh
run.
The competition workbook and CAD-derived network accompany this local research package.
The original CAD drawing and coordinate-transform key do not. No public repository, release
permission, or independently verified mine provenance is asserted. The source geometry is an
anonymized derivative supplied for this study. The manuscript is a working research draft; the
code, mathematical development, and exposition were prepared with AI assistance and require
author review before external circulation.
