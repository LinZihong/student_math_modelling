Buying warning-delay tolerance
with fixed mine escape routes
Working preprint — October 25, 2026
Abstract
A regional flood alarm may tell a miner which part of a mine is affected without identifying
the exact inrush point or how long flooding has been underway. We study an evacuation plan
that remains implementable with that information: one route, fixed in advance for each worker
location and alarm region, followed immediately upon receipt. The question is how much
extra distance is required to guarantee escape across specified source scenarios and notification
delays. Under first-in–first-out tunnel travel and permanent closures, a target delay reduces to a
common-route feasibility problem at one departure time. We solve it by multi-scenario label
search and distinguish lack of a common route from physical infeasibility even with perfect source
information. In an exploratory volume-transfer model, an exhaustive node-source study of one
competition-network region raises the number of outside-region origins supporting a 30-minute
delay from 268 of 548 on the baseline shortest routes to 501 with a 20% distance allowance,
and 545 without a distance limit. A separate two-network screen shows why source coverage
matters: improvements on design sources can disappear or reverse on unseen sources. These are
finite-scenario, model-relative guarantees, not calibrated mine-safety predictions. The results
support evaluating fixed evacuation plans by the delay they can withstand and the distance that
guarantee costs, while making their information and hazard assumptions explicit.
1 Introduction
An emergency route map is useful only if the person reading it has enough information to choose
the prescribed route. An instruction that requires the exact flood source or the true onset time may
be unavailable when an alarm reaches an underground worker. A simpler instruction is conceivable:
“for an alarm from the eastern region, take this marked route.” Such a route must cope with several
possible water sources and with water that has already been spreading before notification.
Mine water inrush is a sudden entry of water into underground workings, for example from
a water-bearing formation or flooded void. Connected roadways provide both escape paths and
channels for the water. Flooding can slow movement and eventually make passages impassable. A
shortest route on a dry map can therefore be fragile even when a slightly longer alternative remains
usable.
Existing mine-inrush research computes earliest-arrival paths in evolving water conditions [1].
Sensor-location research addresses how rapidly an inrush can be detected, explicitly allowing water
to spread before reaching a monitor [2]. Between detection and dynamic routing lies a practical
planning question:
Given only a regional alarm, can a worker follow a route fixed before the accident and
still escape after a specified notification delay? How much detour is needed to obtain
that guarantee?
1
The route may depend on the worker’s starting location and the reported region. It may not depend
on the unreported exact source, the unknown event age, or future observations. The worker leaves
immediately after receiving the alarm.
We formulate a guarantee-first problem: for a target delay, find a common feasible route across
the source scenarios, then minimize distance within that feasible set. This order matters. A 5%
detour allowance is an experimental comparison, not a presumed operational requirement. We also
compute feasibility without a distance limit and report the resulting burden.
The central empirical finding is that fixed regional routes can substantially outperform dry
shortest routes within the stated model, but their apparent robustness depends strongly on which
sources are included. For one region, all 113 candidate source nodes are evaluated against all 548
eligible outside-region origins. A 20% allowance increases the number supporting a 30-minute delay
from 268 to 501. Yet a route designed using eight sources and successful on another eight supports
only about 1.5 minutes when a missed source is added. Thus a convincing plan needs both a route
and an explicit account of the hazards against which it was checked.
Our contribution is a decision formulation, an auditable finite-scenario computation, and an
investigation of this coverage problem. The underlying tools—FIFO path propagation, resource
dominance, and robust scenario constraints—are established optimization ideas. We do not claim a
new general shortest-path algorithm or the first use of robust routing. Preparing several policies
and selecting one after information arrives is studied inK-adaptability [3]; here we deliberately
specify the smaller information set available at the alarm. Section 3.1 also explains why a menu
indexed only by delay does not increase the maximum guaranteed delay under our assumptions.
This study grew out of a mine-inrush modeling competition [4]. It uses a competition network
and a network extracted from a CAD drawing, but does not assume the reader has seen either
the competition solution or the extraction files. The flood calculation is an uncalibrated volume-
transfer surrogate. The numerical results establish properties of specified hazard schedules, not the
effectiveness of a real mine’s emergency plan.
2 What the alarm reveals
2.1 Three timestamps, not one
Let t0 denote physical inrush onset,tdet detection, andtrec receipt of the alarm by the worker. If
immediate departure follows receipt, the relevant event age is
d=t rec−t0 = (t det−t0) + (trec−tdet).
Any additional response time can be included ind. A recorded detection timestamp can help
identify the second term, provided it is communicated and clocks are comparable. It does not by
itself identify the first term. Water may take time to reach a sensor; the sensor-placement study
of Wu et al. [2] explicitly models detection within a prescribed time after inrush. Conversely, a
witnessed event or a sufficiently informative monitoring system might constrain onset closely. We
do not assume that every mine knows, or that every mine fails to know, the true delay.
Our base information model supplies a regionR and an assurance that departure occurs no
later than a planning horizonτafter onset. The worker need not know the realizedd. The planner
asks whether the prescribed route works forevery0≤d≤τ. This is a design specification, not an
instruction to wait after receiving an alarm.
Warnings based on precursor risk before an inrush are a different information process. They
are not modeled by simply assigning a negative notification delay. Likewise, the region reported by

a sensor need not coincide with the physical source region. We assume the alarm’s source-region
interpretation is correct; uncertain classification would require enlarging the scenario set.
2.2 The scenario set is part of the claim
For a reported regionR, letSR be the specified set of possible hazard scenarios. A scenario may
contain a source location, flow history, hydraulic parameters, and their resulting tunnel conditions.
The main experiments vary source location while holding the other parameters fixed; a later
sensitivity changes inflow.
No probability distribution over accidents is inferred from a list of nodes. A statement that a
route works in all sampled scenarios is a finite-set guarantee. It becomes a guarantee over a physical
region only if the set or an accompanying bound adequately represents that region. In particular,
checking every graph node does not check every location along every tunnel.
A route assignment is writtenP (v,R ), wherev is a worker origin. It is chosen offline. During
an incident, the observed region selects the assignment, and the worker follows it without receiving
the exact source. This is the mathematical version of a region-indexed emergency route map. We
do not study how such a map should be designed or tested with users.

3.1 When knowing the delay helps
Suppose the source set remainsSR, the path constraints do not change with the reported delay, and
the objective is to guarantee escape for every delay up toτ.
Corollary 1(Delay-only menus do not extend the guarantee).Under Proposition 1, allowing a
worker to select among finitely many precomputed routes using the exact delay does not make a
targetτfeasible if no single common route can achieve it.
Proof. The route selected atτmust work for all sources inSR. By Proposition 1, that same route
works at all earlier delays. It is therefore a valid single-route plan for the whole interval.
The corollary also holds under a common length cap. Delay information can still reduce walking
burden: an early alarm may justify a shorter route while a late alarm requires a detour. With an
estimated age interval[d−,d +], a route safe atd+ covers that interval under the same assumptions.
Two or three age bands could approximate this distance benefit.
The conclusion changes if observations also narrow the possible sources, or if feasibility is not
downward closed. More informative sensor observations may eliminate scenarios and genuinely
enlarge the feasible set. We therefore keep delay-indexed menus outside the main model rather than
introduce additional policies without establishing what information selects them.

5 Networks, hazard schedules, and experiment design
5.1 Two networks and illustrative alarm regions
The competition network has 664 nodes, 978 undirected tunnels, and two escape endpoints after
insertion of the supplied reference source location. The CAD-derived network has 1,592 nodes, 2,084
tunnels, and four designated shaft-bottom endpoints. Widths are 4 and approximately 4.99 meters,
respectively. A shaft-bottom endpoint terminates our route calculation; reaching the surface is not
simulated.
The CAD graph was extracted using geometric snapping, intersection splitting, and shaft
connectors. Roof elevations serve as a proxy for relative floor elevations under a constant-height
assumption. Those choices require engineering validation. The competition graph is also a model
input, not a surveyed accident reconstruction.
For each network, medianx andy coordinates define four geographic quadrants. These illustrate
coarse source-region alarms. They are not asserted to be actual sensor sectors, geologically equiprob-
able source areas, or operational boundaries. Candidate sources exclude escape endpoints and the
auxiliary reference source node. Worker-source coincidence is excluded in the selected screen; the
dense experiment considers workers outside its entire source region.
5.2 The flood surrogate and its limits
The inherited event-driven model accounts for inflow volume on the tunnel graph. Water is split
among available level or downhill outlets; uphill outlets become active when those choices are
unavailable. Covered tunnels transmit water or store it according to a propagation tree, and
saturated branches cause backing up. A tunnel’s depth proxy is stored volume divided by width
and length. Stored volume never decreases.
The base source supplies0.5 m3 s−1, with a nominal 3-meter tunnel height. A passage becomes
wet at its modeled first-entry time and closes permanently at mean depth 0.3 meters. This threshold
is a modeling convention, not a universal human-safety limit. Travel speed changes from dry to wet;
movement with and against the assigned flow can differ. For a tunnel of lengthL, wetting timew,
and closureb, arrival is calculated exactly by traversing the dry portion atvd and the remaining
distance atvw, then checking arrival byb. All experiments usevd≥vw > 0, giving FIFO behavior.
The initial two-network screen retains the competition speeds4/2/1 m s−1for dry travel, move-
ment with the flow, and movement against it, respectively. Because the dry speed is high for
sustained evacuation, the principal dense-source and all-origin experiments use1.3/0.7/0.5 m s−1.
These are sensitivity values, not fitted pedestrian laws.
Volume-accounting residuals are checked, but conservation does not establish realistic flood
arrival. The model omits resolved momentum, spatial depth profiles, junction losses, pumping,
debris, velocity-dependent stability, and human congestion. Its propagation-tree and event-order
conventions can matter. No hydraulic calibration is claimed for either network. We therefore
interpret the results as a route-planning study conditional on hazard schedules.
5.3 Discovery, source coverage, and broader checks
The experiment has three stages, which must not be mistaken for independent replications.
1. Two-network screen.In each of eight network-region combinations, a fixed random seed
selects eight design sources and eight distinct held-out sources. All eligible origins are screened
on the design scenarios. Up to 12 whose baseline route has a deadline between zero and one
hour, plus up to four with an initially infeasible baseline, are randomly selected per region.
6
This gives 123 origin-region pairs: 96 baseline-feasible and 27 initially infeasible. Budgets are
0, 5, 10, and 20%, later extended to 50, 100%, and no cap.
2. Dense-source investigation.A promising competition-network case, origin P0104 with a
southeast alarm, is selected after viewing the screen. All 113 eligible source nodes in that
quadrant are simulated. The original route is checked and then redesigned against the complete
node set. Twenty-four randomly selected tunnel-midpoint sources are subsequently tested
against the fixed redesigned route, without retraining it.
3. All-origin target study .Holding that 113-source set fixed, all 548 eligible origins outside the
quadrant are evaluated at 10-, 20-, and 30-minute delay targets using the slower speeds. This
removes the favorable-origin selection within this one region, but does not establish prevalence
across regions or mines.
The initial protocol and source identities were recorded before those runs. Dense-source checks and
the all-origin study are adaptive follow-ups, not a preregistered external confirmation. Node counts
describe designed cases; they are not probabilities of surviving an accident.

6.2 A representative route and a non-smooth trade-off
For P0104, the dry shortest route is 2,701.36 meters. Across the dense source set, that route fails
even at onset in some scenarios. A common route of 2,768.58 meters—67.23 meters, or 2.49%,
longer—has deadline 11.94 minutes at the slower speeds. With the competition speeds, the same
route supports 16.47 minutes.
The benefit is not a smooth reward for every extra meter. Allowing 20% yields a 3,240.94-meter
route and an 80.16-minute common deadline under the slower speeds. Budgets of 50 and 100%
return the same optimum. With no distance limit, the optimum reaches 360.17 minutes, but
requires a 17.21-kilometer route. Those values were computed without the one-hour optimization
cap and bracketed to less than 0.5 seconds; the long-time flood predictions remain particularly
model dependent.
The 24 new midpoint sources do not reduce the 11.94-minute guarantee of the 2,768.58-meter
route. Their worst deadline exceeds 68 minutes at the slower speeds. This is useful stress evidence
against a purely node-specific success, but it does not establish coverage of every interior point. Nor
were all routes in Table 1 tested against these midpoint sources.
6.3 Eight successful test sources can miss a damaging source
The dense case also provides a warning about evaluation. Using eight design sources at the
competition speeds, the originally selected route supported the one-hour design horizon. It also
supported that horizon for all eight held-out sources. Yet a further source, P0172, reduced its
deadline to 89.88 seconds. Holding the route fixed while expanding source coverage exposed the
failure; changing paths separately for each test source would have concealed it.

6.5 Fixed-route sensitivity to flow and event ordering
To separate plan sensitivity from the benefit of reoptimization, we retained the 545 uncapped routes
that meet the 30-minute target in the dense-source study and evaluated the same routes under
altered schedules. Their speeds remain1.3/0.7/0.5 m s−1.
For this constant-inflow volume-transfer model, multiplying inflow by a factor rescales event
times inversely; this homogeneity was separately checked on recomputed flows in the original model
audit. Applying it toQ = 0.25, 0.5, 1.0 m3 s−1, all 545 routes retain the target at the first two flows,
but only 329 retain it at the doubled flow. This is an internal model sensitivity, not evidence that
real flood dynamics scale uniformly with inflow.
We also reran all 113 source simulations with the model’s alternative ordering of simultaneous
edge events. All 545 fixed routes retained the 30-minute guarantee under that convention. This
check rules out a failure under the tested ordering change; it does not validate the propagation-tree
physics or establish invariance to arbitrary junction models.
The flow result is consequential: a source-location guarantee at one prescribed inflow does not
silently extend to unknown inflow magnitude. A practical scenario set would need to include such
uncertainty explicitly. The current headline counts should retain their fixed-flow qualifier.
7 Interpretation and limitations
The main result concerns how to evaluate an implementable plan. Regional information can support
a common route that accepts more distance in exchange for a specified delay guarantee. The
all-origin study shows a substantial improvement over dry shortest routes in one modeled region.
The held-out and dense-source comparisons show that the guarantee is only as broad as its hazard
set.
Knowing the event age has a narrower role than it might first appear. Under downward-closed
feasibility, a route that survives the latest admissible notification also survives earlier notifications.
Delay-only route menus can save walking distance for early alarms, but cannot extend the worst-case
guarantee while the source set and constraints remain unchanged. If future observations reduce the
possible sources, adaptive routing can have a different value. A sensor-informed extension should
model the observation process rather than grant the routing method access to the true simulated
source.
Four limitations prevent direct deployment conclusions.
1. Hydraulic credibility .The schedules come from a volume-transfer surrogate, not a calibrated
transient hydraulic solver. Average tunnel depth, propagation-tree junctions, permanent clo-
sure, and uncertain geometry may alter which branch is preferable. More routing experiments
cannot cure that limitation.
2. Coverage and selection.Dense node coverage is available for one region; the rest of the
two-network experiment uses small source samples. The dense case was selected adaptively, and
the all-origin population excludes the source region. Interior sources, multiple simultaneous
inrushes, uncertain alarm classification, and general flow histories remain outside the guarantee.
3. Human and exit assumptions.Speed is prescribed without fatigue, group interaction,
visibility, or stability modeling. Long routes may be unacceptable. Reaching a designated
network exit is treated as completion; ascent and refuge operations are not modeled.
10
4. Monotone time structure.Endpoint reduction and the delay-menu corollary require
downward-closed path feasibility. Receding hazards, temporary barriers, waiting in refuges, or
non-FIFO conditions may require an interval-wide treatment and can give time information
additional value.
The numerical precision of a deadline bracket should not be confused with physical accuracy.
Seconds in the solver output document optimization consistency; they do not warrant second-level
evacuation instructions. Similarly, improvement counts quantify this graph experiment rather than
an accident survival probability.
8 Conclusion
A regional evacuation plan can be evaluated by a concrete requirement: one route must remain
feasible for every included source when the alarm arrives as late as a specified time. In the studied
hazard schedules, useful guarantees often require modest detours, while some origins need much
longer routes or cannot share a feasible route across scenarios at all.
The next research priority is to preserve this information-constrained question while replacing
uncertain schedules with better-supported ones. Which local branches determine the common
deadline under a transient hydraulic model? How densely must source locations be represented
before the guarantee stabilizes? Can sensor observations distinguish the scenarios that demand
incompatible routes? These questions connect the route map to the information and physical
evidence needed to justify it.
