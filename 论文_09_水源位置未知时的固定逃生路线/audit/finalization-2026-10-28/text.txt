Planning Mine Escape Routes
with Zone-Based Flood Alarms
Jingchao Wang
Abstract
Mine escape routes are fixed in advance, usually as the shortest way to a shaft, but when
water breaks in, the alarm reports only which zone of the mine is flooding. We ask what a
fixed escape plan needs to know about where the water enters. On a 248-km tunnel network
from a real coal mine, we simulate a flood from each of its 1,587 junctions and compare three
plans: the shortest route, a simple rule that avoids the alarm zone, and the shortest route
that is guaranteed for every possible source in the zone, which we compute exactly. Three
findings stand out. First, shortest routes fail not because the warning is late but because they
converge on the shafts, where the water arrives first; with four alarm zones and a 30-minute
delay, they can be guaranteed for only 59% of (worker, flood) pairs. Second, the shortest
route that survives every possible source in the zone raises this to 79%; a simple rule that
steers clear of the alarm zone, without checking the flood, reaches 69% but with much longer
walks. Third, almost all of the remaining gap to the 99.8% possible with the exact source is
a lack of information, not of time: finer alarm zones close it, and drawing zone boundaries
through the shafts is worth about as much as doubling the number of zones. The results rest
on one mine and an uncalibrated flood model.
1 Introduction
A water inrush happens when groundwater, or water stored in old workings, suddenly breaks into
an operating mine. It is among the most dangerous accidents underground [4, 14, 15], because
the water and the miners move through the same tunnels. A large mine can have hundreds of
kilometers of tunnels connected to the surface by only a few shafts. Water entering at one point
runs downhill, fills low sections, and can cut off passages far from where it started. Workers who
are still underground have to reach a shaft before the tunnels on their way become too deep to
pass.
Escape routes are therefore planned in advance. Each work area is assigned a route to a
shaft, the route is marked underground, and workers practice it in drills [12]. In an emergency
people follow what they have practiced; nobody computes a fresh route for each worker. The
natural route to practice is the shortest one.
The difficulty is that a route fixed in advance cannot know where the water will come from.
Even after the alarm sounds, the source is usually known only roughly. A water-level sensor or a
report from a work face tells the control room that water has appeared in some part of the mine,
and the alarm passes that zone on to workers. A route that is safe for a flood in one corner of
that zone may run straight into a flood from another corner. The warning may also arrive late,
because water can travel some distance before it reaches a sensor.
Earlier work has treated these two sides separately. Escape-route studies find the fastest
path through a mine whose flooding has been simulated in advance [16], and sensor studies place
water-level sensors so that an inrush is detected quickly [17]. What connects them is the plan
itself: what route should a worker be told to take when the alarm reports only a zone? This
paper asks what a fixed escape plan needs to know about where the water comes from, and what
it should do with the little that an alarm reveals. We break the question into three parts.
1
1. How often does the shortest route fail when only the zone is known, and why?
2. How much better can a route do if it is checked against every possible source in the zone,
how much walking does it cost, and does a simple rule do as well?
3. When no fixed route works, is what is missing better information about the source, or an
earlier warning?
We study these questions on a tunnel network taken from the plan of a real coal mine, with
1,592 junctions and about 248km of tunnels. For each of its 1,587 junctions we simulate a flood
that starts there, using a simple model of how water spreads and when each tunnel becomes
impassable. We then compare plans for every starting point and every alarm zone. Besides the
shortest route, we test a rule that a planner could apply with a map alone: avoid the tunnels of
the alarm zone. Against both we set therobust route, the shortest route that reaches a shaft
for every possible source in the zone. Finding it means searching for one route that works in
hundreds of floods at once, which we do exactly. Finally, we vary the alarm itself, from no
location information at all to 128 small zones, and compare the result with the ceiling set by
knowing the exact source.
The main findings are as follows. With four alarm zones and a 30-minute warning delay,
shortest routes can be guaranteed for 59% of all (worker, flood) pairs, the avoid-the-zone rule
for 69%, and robust routes for 79%, whereas knowing the exact source would allow 99.8%. The
shortest routes do not fail because the warning is late: most fail even for a worker who leaves at
once, and they are cut within a few hundred meters of the shafts, where routes from all over the
mine meet. The avoid-the-zone rule fixes many of the same workers, but often by sending them
on very long detours that a robust route avoids. Nearly all of the remaining gap is a matter of
information rather than time, and it closes as the alarm zones become finer. Where the zone
boundaries fall matters as much as how many zones there are. A zone that contains the whole
shaft area is a weak point that more zones elsewhere do not remove, and two zones divided at
the shafts protect 52% of pairs, against 31–36% for two zones of equal size.
The numbers come from a single mine and an uncalibrated flood model, so they show what
the planning method can reveal rather than how safe this particular mine is. Section 8 discusses
the limits.
Related work.Wu et al. [16] find earliest-arrival escape paths through a mine network whose
roadways flood over time, using the results of a flood simulation for a given inrush, and Zhang et
al. [19] model the spreading itself in more detail. Water-level sensors can be placed to detect an
inrush quickly [17]; our results suggest that where zone boundaries fall relative to the shafts also
matters for what the detection can be used for. Evacuation planning on networks more generally
has a large optimization literature [1].
Our computational tools come from time-dependent and robust routing. Shortest paths with
travel times that change over time go back to Dreyfus [5]; when arriving earlier never means
leaving later, arrival times can be compared directly and label-setting search stays exact [8, 13].
Comparing partial routes on several criteria at once follows multicriteria shortest-path methods
[9], including time-dependent versions [6]. Uncertain travel times have been handled through
expected arrival times [10] and through robust shortest paths that guard against the worst of
several scenarios [18, 3]; Ben-Tal et al. [2] survey robust optimization in general. Our search adds
a scenario only when a candidate route fails it, in the spirit of cutting-set methods [11]. Choosing
among prepared plans after part of the uncertainty is revealed is studied asK-adaptability [7];
here the revealed part is the alarm zone, and we study how its resolution changes what a plan
can guarantee.
Section 2 sets up the problem and Section 3 explains how robust routes are computed.
Sections 4–6 answer the three questions in turn, Section 7 tests how the answers change with the
inflow and the set of possible sources, and Section 8 discusses limitations.

2 The planning problem
2.1 The mine
Our test case is a tunnel network extracted from an anonymized CAD plan of a working longwall
coal mine (Figure 1). It has 1,592 junctions and 2,084 tunnels with a total length of about
248km. A main roadway runs diagonally across the mine, and longwall panels branch off it on
both sides. The seam dips gently: tunnel floors in the west lie about 80m higher than those
in the far northeast, so water tends to run east and northeast. All four shafts stand on the
main roadway, within about half a kilometer of each other near the middle of the mine. Once a
worker reaches the bottom of a shaft we count the escape as complete; the trip up the shaft is
not modeled.
To build the network we joined nearly touching lines on the plan, split lines where they cross,
and connected the shafts to the tunnels. The plan gives roof elevations, which we use as relative
floor elevations by assuming all tunnels have the same height.

2.2 Sources and alarm zones
Any junction except the four shafts and one auxiliary node can be the place where water enters,
which we call thesource. That gives 1,587 possible sources, and the same 1,587 junctions are the
possible starting points of workers. Each flood has exactly one source, and we treat all sources
as equally likely.
The alarm divides the mine into zones and reports the zone that contains the source. It does
not report the source itself. We use two kinds of zones. The first is the fourquadrantsformed by
the medianx and y coordinates of the junctions (Figure 2); they are the simplest possible split.
Their boundaries happen to cross right at the shaft cluster, a fact that turns out to matter. The
second is a family of finer zones that we use to measure the value of better information. We cut
the mine along a straight line into two halves that contain the same tunnel length, cut each half
along the perpendicular direction, and so on, which gives 2, 4, 8, ..., 128 zones of roughly equal
tunnel length. With 128 zones, a zone holds about 2km of tunnel. To check that the results do
not depend on where the cuts fall, we repeat the construction with the first cut at three angles,
0, 60 and 120 degrees. In a real mine, the zones would follow the placement of water sensors;
ours are geometric stand-ins that let us vary the resolution in a controlled way.
2.3 How late the warning can be
The alarm also comes late. Water may travel some distance before it reaches a sensor, and
passing the warning on to workers takes more time. If the inrush starts at timet0 and the worker
leaves on receiving the alarm at timetrec, the worker sets off a delayd =trec−t0 after the inrush
began. The planner cannot knowd in advance, so we ask for routes that work for every delay up
to a targetτ. Our main target is 30 minutes, and we vary it from zero to two hours. The target
is about how late the worker may set off, not about how long the walk takes.
4
2.4 The flood and walking model
To check a route, we need to know, for each source, when each tunnel first gets wet and when it
becomes too deep to pass. We compute these times with an event-driven model that tracks the
volume of water in every tunnel, developed for a modeling-competition problem on mine inrush
[4]. More detailed models add pipe-flow dynamics, energy losses at junctions and pumping [19];
we use the simpler model because the routing questions need flood times for all 1,587 sources.
Water arriving at a junction divides equally among the level or downhill tunnels leaving it, and
goes uphill only when no such tunnel is available. A thin layer of water covers a tunnel before
the water moves on beyond it. Dead-end branches fill up, and water then backs up behind them.
The depth in a tunnel is its stored volume divided by its floor area. Appendix B gives the full
rules.
The main runs use a constant inflow of0.5 m3/s, tunnels 4.99m wide and 3m high, and a
0.1m water layer at the advancing front. A tunnel closes for good when its mean depth reaches
0.3m. Workers walk at 1.3m/s in dry tunnels; in wet tunnels they walk at 0.7m/s with the flow
and 0.5m/s against it. If water reaches a tunnel while a worker is inside it, the worker slows
down from that moment. These values define a test case; they are not measured walking speeds
or a validated safety threshold.
One property of this model simplifies everything that follows. Water never recedes, so a
worker who enters a tunnel earlier never leaves it later. It follows that a route that works for
a worker who sets off atτalso works for any earlier departure, and checking a whole range of
delays only requires checking the latest one (Appendix A).
2.5 Four plans
A plan assigns each starting pointv and each alarm zoneZ one route, fixed in advance. Every
route in every plan ends at the bottom of one of the four shafts; the plans differ in which shaft
they choose and how they get there. We compare four plans.
Shortest route. Every worker takes the shortest route to the nearest shaft, whatever the alarm
says. This is the natural default.
Avoid-the-zone rule.Among all routes to a shaft, the worker takes the shortest of those that
spend the least distance in tunnels touching the alarm zone. A planner could apply this rule
with a map alone. The rule does not look at the flood when it picks a route; we check its
routes afterwards, with the same test as every other plan.
Robust route. Among all routes to a shaft, the shortest one that gets the worker out for every
possible source in the zone, checked one flood at a time, and for every delay up toτ. Unlike
the avoid-the-zone rule, it uses the flood model to pick the route, and it may cross tunnels of
the zone that the water reaches late. Finding it requires the search described in Section 3.
When no such route exists, the starting point cannot be protected by any fixed plan for that
zone.
Exact source. For comparison only, the best route for a worker who is told the exact source.
No alarm system delivers this, but it marks a ceiling that no plan can beat.

2.6 How we measure protection
We say a planprotectsa starting point for a zone when the route it assigns works for every
source in the zone and every delay up toτ. For the quadrants, we count protected starting
points outside and inside each zone separately. Workers inside the zone may be standing next to
the source, and their prospects are much worse.
To compare zones of different sizes, we also use a whole-mine measure. Consider every pair
of a starting point and a source, 1,587 by 1,587 pairs in all. A pair is protected if the plan’s
route for the source’s zone is guaranteed for that starting point. The share of protected pairs
is the chance that a randomly placed worker is protected in a flood from a randomly placed
source. Each unprotected pair falls into one of two groups. If even the exact source would not
help, because the worker has no way out of that flood after a delay ofτ, we call the pairno way
out. Otherwise, a route exists for that flood, but no single route works for the whole zone; we
call the pairinformation-limited, because a finer alarm could help it.
3 Finding a route that works for every source
Computing the best route for each source separately and comparing the answers does not give
one route that works for all of them, so the search has to consider all floods in the zone at once.
We do this exactly for a finite set of sources.
One clock for each flood.Imagine that a partial route carries one clock for each flood,
showing when the worker would reach the current junction in that flood. The search storeslabels
at each junction, each recording a route length and the arrival times in all floods. Extending
a route along a tunnel updates every clock, using that flood’s wetting time, closure time and
walking speed. If the tunnel would already be closed in any flood when the worker gets there,
the extension is discarded. If one label at a junction is no longer than another and arrives no
later in every flood, the other label can be dropped, since arriving earlier never hurts; this is the
dominance rule of multicriteria shortest-path search [9, 6]. The search expands labels in order
of length plus the remaining dry distance to a shaft, so the first complete route it finds is the
shortest route that works in every flood.
Adding floods only when needed.A zone can hold hundreds of sources, and most of them
do not matter for a given starting point. We start with the source that is worst for the shortest
route, find the shortest route that works for it, and check that route against every source in the
zone with a fast backward calculation. If some source defeats the route, we add that source and
search again; otherwise the route is optimal for the whole zone. If no route works even for the
sources added so far, none works for the zone. This is a cutting-set scheme [11] with the flood
model as the oracle that finds a defeating source. The procedure is exact and ends after at most
as many rounds as there are sources (Appendix A).
Scale.Our experiments solve this problem 2,558,244 times, once for every starting point, zone,
delay target and inflow considered. With time limits of three seconds per search and 30 seconds
per query, all but seven finished. We re-solved those seven, all for workers inside zones that cover
at least a quarter of the mine, with a limit of one hour. Each finished within 18 seconds and
showed that no common route exists, so every result reported here is exact. We checked the
implementation against brute-force enumeration on small networks, confirmed that the results
obey the logical relations that must hold between the four plans, and walked a sample of routes
forward against every source with separate code (Appendix C).

4 Why the shortest route fails
We begin with the four quadrant zones and a 30-minute delay target. Table 1 gives, for each zone,
how many starting points outside it each plan protects. Workers inside the zone are discussed at
the end of Section 5.
The shortest route works poorly for alarms in the two western zones. For a northwest alarm
it protects 720 of the 1,243 starting points outside the zone (58%), and for a southwest alarm
659 of 1,139 (58%). The southeast and northeast zones lose far fewer: 45 and 63 starting points.
These failures are not caused by the late warning. Of the 523 northwest failures, 473 would
happen even if the worker left at the moment the inrush began, and the same holds for 441 of
the 480 southwest failures (Figure 3b). Across all four zones, 91% of the shortest routes that fail
would fail without any delay at all.
They fail because of where the shafts are. To see where, we followed each failing shortest
route through the flood that defeats it and recorded where the worker is stopped. For a northwest
alarm, the median stopping point lies 130m along the tunnels from a shaft, and 508 of the 523 lie
within a kilometer of one; for a southwest alarm the median is 280m. All four shafts stand close
together on the main roadway, right where the northwest and southwest zones meet (Figure 2).
Water from a source in either zone runs into the junctions around the shafts, where routes from
every part of the mine converge. A worker far from the flood, walking the shortest route, walks
into it at the very end.
Seen over the whole mine, a shortest-route plan with quadrant alarms can guarantee an
escape for 58.7% of all (worker, flood) pairs. The guarantee is a demanding standard, because
it must hold for every source in the zone. A complementary measure asks how often the route
actually fails for the source that occurs. By this measure the shortest route traps 2.8% of workers
when the warning comes 30 minutes late, which is 13 times the 0.2% who would have no way out
even with the exact source known.

6 Information or time?
6.1 The gap is a matter of information
Under quadrant alarms, 21.2% of (worker, flood) pairs remain unprotected by robust routes at a
30-minute target. Only 0.2% have no way out, in the sense that even the exact source would not
save the worker. The other 21.0% are information-limited: a route exists for that flood, but no
single route works for every source in the zone. Almost the whole gap is therefore a matter of
information.
6.2 Finer zones, and where their boundaries fall
To see how much information is needed, we replaced the quadrants by zones of equal tunnel
length, from a single zone covering the whole mine up to 128 zones (Table 2 and Figure 6a). With
no location information at all, robust routes can be guaranteed for only 10% of pairs. Protection
then rises with the number of zones, to 51–61% with four zones, 82% with sixteen, and 97% with
sixty-four, close to the 99.8% ceiling. The three cutting angles agree within a few points, except
with four, eight and thirty-two zones, where a small shift of the boundaries changes the result by
up to nine points.

The four quadrants stand out. They protect 78.8% of pairs, about as much as sixteen equal
zones and far more than four. The reason is again the shafts. In every equal-length partition
with up to sixteen zones, a single zone contains all 167 junctions within 300m of a shaft. When
that zone raises the alarm, any source in it can flood the approach to every shaft, and robust
routes exist for only 10–33% of starting points. The large step between sixteen and sixty-four
zones is the point at which the boundaries first split the shaft area. The quadrant boundaries
happen to cross right at the shafts, splitting this area almost in half, so that even their worst
alarm leaves 82% of starting points protected.
To test this directly, we drew zones through the shaft cluster on purpose. One straight line
across the main roadway between the shafts gives two zones with two shafts on each side; a
second line along the roadway gives four zones. Two zones divided at the shafts protect 51.7%
of pairs, against 31–36% for two zones of equal tunnel length, and four protect 69.0%, against
51–61% (Figure 6a). Drawing the boundaries through the shafts is worth about as much as
doubling the number of zones. It is not the whole story, since the quadrants, which also split the
shaft area, do better still; where the other boundaries fall matters too.

6.3 Delay makes information more valuable
Delay by itself costs very little in this model. With the exact source known, the share of pairs
with a way out falls only from 100% to 99.8% as the delay grows from zero to 30 minutes, and to
99.5% at two hours (Figure 6b). Under quadrant alarms, the picture is different. Robust routes
protect 96.9% of pairs when workers leave at once, 84.1% at 10 minutes, 78.8% at 30 minutes
and 69.4% at two hours. The information-limited share grows accordingly, from 3% to 30%.
Delay and information therefore interact. A worker who leaves immediately can almost always
find one route that stays ahead of every possible flood in the zone. The longer the warning takes,
the more the floods from different sources have spread, and the less likely it is that one route
avoids all of them. With finer zones the effect shrinks: with 128 zones, protection falls only from
98.9% at 10 minutes to 97.9% at 60 minutes. A faster alarm and a more precise alarm are partly
substitutes, but the precise one is worth more.
7 Sensitivity to inflow and to the set of possible sources
A stronger inflow.Doubling the inflow to1 .0 m3/s halves every flood time. With quadrant
alarms and a 30-minute target, the guaranteed share falls to 56.9% for shortest routes, 66.5%
for the avoid-the-zone rule and 71.6% for robust routes, while the ceiling stays at 99.7%. The
pattern across zone resolutions is unchanged, including the jump at the point where the shaft
area is split (Appendix E). Because flood times scale with the inflow (Appendix B) and walking
is slower in wet tunnels, in this model a route that works at a higher inflow also works at any
lower one, so a plan designed for an upper bound on the inflow covers all smaller inflows.
Fewer possible sources.So far every junction in the reported zone could be the source. In
practice some places are much more likely than others. As a contrasting case, we kept only
junctions within a given distance of the water areas marked on the CAD plan, which we treat
as a rough stand-in for likely sources (Table 3). With sources within 50m of these areas, 211
junctions in all, shortest routes can already be guaranteed for 93.8% of pairs, robust routes
raise this to 97.8%, and the information-limited share is 2.1%. As the radius grows to 1km, the
information-limited share rises to 19.0%, close to the 21.0% for whole zones. The fewer places

the water can come from, the less the alarm needs to say, and the less a robust plan adds. Any
estimate of what rerouting is worth therefore depends on which sources the plan is meant to
withstand.

8 Limitations and implications
The results come from a single mine, so they show what can happen rather than how often it
happens in mines generally. In particular, the strong effect of the shafts depends on this mine’s
layout, with all four shafts close together on one roadway; a mine with shafts far apart would
behave differently. Our zones are geometric stand-ins for sensor zones. We treated every junction
as an equally likely source and did not consider points partway along a tunnel. The counts in
our tables are numbers of junctions, which are not the same as numbers of workers.
The flood model is the main physical limitation. It conserves water volume, but it splits flow
equally at junctions, spreads water along a tree of tunnels, and tracks only the mean depth of
each tunnel. It ignores momentum, changes in depth along a tunnel, losses at junctions, pumping,
debris, and the force of moving water on a person. Its timing has not been calibrated against
any measurement. The extracted geometry and estimated elevations would also need review by
mining engineers. The routing questions are realistic; the flood times are not.
The model of the workers is equally simple. Routes end at the bottom of a shaft, and the
model ignores queues, hoist capacity, fatigue, injury, and the chance of a wrong turn. Some
robust routes are long, and a long route may be safe in the model and still be a poor emergency
instruction.
Within these limits, the study suggests three practical points. First, check the shortest routes
against every source in each alarm zone, not just against the most likely one; a route can fail far
from where the water starts. Second, look at the area around the shafts: if all shaft approaches
lie in one alarm zone, that zone is the weak point of the plan, and splitting it is worth more than
adding zones elsewhere. Third, a simple rule of avoiding the alarm zone captures much of the
benefit of rerouting, but checking routes against a flood model can find much shorter ones and
can show which workers no fixed route can protect.
9 Conclusion
A fixed escape plan has to work without knowing where the water comes from. In the mine we
studied, the shortest routes fail for many workers far from any flood, because the water reaches
the shaft area first. Routes checked against every possible source in the alarm zone repair most
of these failures, and a simple rule of avoiding the zone repairs many of them at a higher walking
cost. What remains is almost entirely a matter of information: alarm zones that narrow down
the source, and in particular zones that separate the approaches to different shafts, would let a
12
fixed plan protect nearly every worker, while an earlier warning helps mainly by making that
information less critical. The natural next steps are to test these findings with a calibrated flood
model and with mines whose shafts are spread out, and to design alarm zones and escape routes
together.

B Flood propagation rules
Each tunnel stores a volume between zero and its capacityWHℓ, whereW = 4.99m andH = 3m.
When water first reaches a level or downhill tunnel, the tunnel receives a layer of volumeWh 0ℓ
with h0 = 0.1m. If its far junction is still dry, the tunnel then passes water on and joins a
propagation tree; if the far junction is already wet, it stores further water. An uphill tunnel fills
from below before passing water to a dry junction above it. At a wet junction, incoming flow
is divided equally among the level or downhill tunnels still available (with a 0.005m elevation
tolerance), and uphill tunnels are used only when none remain. When a dead end fills up, the
tunnel feeding it stops passing water on and stores it instead. Inflow trapped behind full tunnels
is moved through them to the nearest junction that can still store water, and overflow after
saturation is counted in the volume balance. Simultaneous events are ordered by junction and
tunnel identifiers. A tunnel closes when its mean depth reaches 0.3m. Walking switches to wet
speed when water first enters a tunnel; the model does not track a moving front inside a tunnel.
Multiplying a constant inflow by a factor divides every flood event time by the same factor,
so the doubled-inflow floods are the nominal floods with all times halved. A direct rerun for one
source reproduced the scaled times exactly.
C Checks on the implementation
We checked the results in four ways.
1. Brute force on small networks.The search code was tested on 100 small random networks
with three floods each, against enumeration of every simple path; all 400 comparisons of
feasibility and minimum length agreed.
2. Reproduction.For the quadrant zones at 10, 20 and 30 minutes, the current code reproduces
an earlier run of the same 19,044 queries, made with separate driver code, exactly, including
every route length.
3. Logical consistency.Several relations must hold if the search is exact. The robust route is the
shortest protected route, so it must exist whenever the shortest route or the avoid-the-zone
route is protected, and it can be no longer than either. It can exist only if every source in
the zone leaves a way out. Its latest safe departure must reach the target. And because a
route that works for a zone also works for each half of it, every starting point protected for
a zone must be protected for both halves in the next finer partition. All 2,558,244 records
satisfy the first four relations, and all 747 parent–child zone pairs satisfy the last.
4. Independent forward walks.For each quadrant zone we sampled eight robust and eight
zone-avoiding routes and walked each one forward through every flood in its zone, with code
separate from the search. Leaving just before the computed latest departure reached a shaft

in all 23,012 route–source checks, and leaving one second after it failed for at least one source
